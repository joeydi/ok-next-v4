<Lead>
  Your website is doing real work for your organization. It takes donations, collects applications, answers questions
  and makes first impressions. That's a lot to ask of something nobody is watching, and it's why the first step toward
  getting a handle on your digital presence is monitoring.
</Lead>

When a site carries that much weight, you can't afford downtime, lost changes or a broken form. A form that quietly stops sending is a lost donation or an unanswered family. A change that gets overwritten is hours of someone's work gone.

Plenty of websites still run the old way: edits made directly on the live server, a "cowboy coding on production" workflow where the only record of what happened is somebody's memory, and no backup until the day you wish you had one. That worked when a website was a brochure. It doesn't hold up now that it's a front door.

The modern way is a workflow where everything is monitored and traceable. "Observability" is not a pretty word for it, but it's the closest one I have. It comes down to four questions you should be able to answer at any time. What changed? Who changed it, and why? Is everything working? Can we go back? ## Daily snapshots show you what changed

Every day, a snapshot of your site is saved next to the one before it. Scroll back through them and you can watch your website grow up: a new banner, a reworked page, a layout that shifted after an update.

- **A loading timeline.** Each snapshot records how the page loaded, frame by frame, so you can see what a visitor sees while they wait. A page that feels slower gets a day it started.
- **Visual comparison.** Two days sit side by side, and the parts that moved are outlined. The change you'd never spot by clicking around becomes obvious, along with the date it happened.

## A source code repo shows who changed it, and why

A change is only half the story. Behind each one is a person and a reason: a page reworded, a section renamed, a plugin upgraded. Keeping the site's code in a source code repository answers those questions for good.

- **Every change is tracked.** Each edit is saved as its own step, with the exact lines that moved.
- **Every change has context.** Each step carries a note from the person who made it, so "what happened here?" has a one-line answer. Most of the time it's a good one, a deliberate update that's worth remembering.
- **Nothing is lost.** Nobody can overwrite somebody else's work by accident. If an edit turns out to be a mistake, the version before it is still there.

## Analytics and events show you when something breaks

Forms stop sending. Emails bounce. A certificate quietly expires. These rarely announce themselves, and a visitor often notices before anyone inside the organization does.

- **Observe the trends.** Form submissions, traffic and conversions have a normal rhythm. Once you can see it, you can tell when it's changed.
- **Spot issues early.** When a contact form that usually gets a dozen submissions a day gets none, that's the signal, and it arrives with a plain note about where the cause likely sits. Sometimes it's the site and sometimes it's the mail provider. Either way, you hear about it first.

<PullQuote>Most of the time, what you'll see is *a whole lot of green dials.*</PullQuote>

That's the goal. Less checking, more green dials.

## Rolling backups let you get it back

Backups are the quiet half of all this, and the part you hope never to use.

- **Peace of mind.** Offsite copies roll forward on a schedule, and they're checked to make sure they actually restore. You don't have to wonder whether one exists.
- **An audit trail.** Together with the repo and the snapshots, backups give you a record of where the site has been. If you ever need to return to an earlier moment, or show someone what the site looked like on a given date, it's there.
