<Lead>
Your website does real work for your organization. It takes donations, collects applications, answers questions and makes first impressions. That's a lot to ask of something nobody is watching. And it's why the first step toward getting a handle on your digital presence is monitoring.
</Lead>

A form that quietly stops sending can mean a lost donation or an unanswered family. A change that gets overwritten can mean hours of someone's work gone. And downtime has a way of happening at exactly the wrong moment.

Plenty of websites still run the old way: edits made directly on the live server, a "cowboy coding on production" workflow where the only record of what happened is somebody's memory, and no backup until the day you wish you had one. That worked when a website was a brochure. It doesn't hold up now that it's a front door.

The modern approach is a workflow where changes are visible, problems are caught early, and nothing important depends on somebody remembering what happened.

"Observability" is not a pretty word for it, but it's the closest one I have. It comes down to four questions you should be able to answer at any time: What changed? Who changed it, and why? Is everything working? Can we go back?

## Daily snapshots show you what changed

Every day, a snapshot of your site is saved next to the one before it. Scroll back through them and you can watch your website grow up: a new banner, a reworked page, a layout that shifted after an update.

- **A loading timeline.** Each snapshot records how the page loaded, frame by frame, so you can see what a visitor sees while they wait. A page that feels slower gets a date you can trace it back to.
- **Visual comparison.** Two days sit side by side, and the parts that moved are outlined. The change you'd never spot by clicking around becomes obvious, along with the date it happened.

## A source code repo shows who changed it, and why

A visual change is only half the story. Behind it might be a developer fixing a bug, a new feature going live, or a plugin being upgraded. Keeping the site's code in a source code repository gives those changes a history.

- **Every code change is tracked.** Each committed change is saved as its own step, with the exact lines that moved.
- **Every change has context.** Each commit carries a note explaining what changed and why, so "what happened here?" has a one-line answer. Most of the time it's a good one, a deliberate update that's worth remembering.
- **Recover when something gets overwritten.** If an edit turns out to be a mistake, or someone's work gets overwritten, the earlier version is still there. You can find it and put it back.

## Analytics and events show you when something breaks

Forms stop sending. Emails bounce. A certificate quietly expires. These problems rarely announce themselves, and a visitor often notices before anyone inside the organization does.

Analytics tells you how many people visit. Events tell you what they do once they're there, and they're what make a quiet failure visible.

- **Track the events that matter.** A form submitted, a donation completed, a newsletter signup confirmed, a file downloaded. Each one is recorded as it happens, so the actions your site exists for are counted instead of guessed at.
- **Observe the trends.** Events and traffic have a normal rhythm. Once you can see it, you can tell when something changes.
- **Spot issues early.** When an event that normally happens stops happening, that's a signal worth investigating. A contact form that usually gets a dozen submissions a day suddenly gets none. Sometimes the problem is on the site, sometimes it's the mail provider. Either way, the data gives you somewhere to start.
- **Get alerted when your site goes down.** Automated uptime checks keep watch around the clock and send an alert when your site stops responding. No waiting for a visitor to report it.

All of that watching adds up to something quieter than you might expect. You aren't checking dashboards every morning. The checks run on their own and only speak up when something is off.

## Rolling backups let you get it back

Backups are the quiet half of all this, and the part you hope never to use.

- **Peace of mind.** Offsite backups run on a schedule and are checked to make sure they actually restore. You don't have to wonder whether a working copy exists.
- **A history worth keeping.** Daily backups are retained for a month, monthly backups for a year, and yearly backups indefinitely. Together with the repo and visual snapshots, they give you a record of where the site has been, and a way back when you need one.

## Look back

It's nice to be able to look back, even if only to see how far you've come. The launches, the growth, the years of steady work, all on one timeline. Most organizations have never seen their website that way.

And when something does go wrong, you don't have to guess what happened or hope someone kept a copy. You have a record, a starting point, and a way forward.
